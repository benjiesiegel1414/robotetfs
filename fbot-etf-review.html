FBOT ETF Review: Fidelity's Actively Managed Automation Fund

FBOT is Fidelity's actively managed automation ETF: about 50 stocks from Deere to TSMC for 0.50%. Here is what it owns, how it has done and who it fits.

By Benjie Siegel · Published November 23, 2026 · Updated November 23, 2026 · 11 min read

Key takeaways

  • $FBOT is the Fidelity Disruptive Automation ETF. It started life as a mutual fund on April 16, 2020 and was reorganized into an ETF on June 9, 2023, with a 0.50% expense ratio.
  • Unlike $BOTZ, $ROBO or $IBOT, it does not track an index. Fidelity's managers pick roughly 50 stocks and must keep at least 80% of assets in what they call disruptive automation companies.
  • On Fidelity's September 30, 2026 fact sheet it held 48 stocks with about $213 million in assets. The top 10 were 40% of the fund, led by TSMC, Deere, Nvidia and Teradyne.
  • Through September 30, 2026 it returned 16.62% a year at NAV since inception and 19.22% over one year, but only 7.36% a year over five years versus 11.94% for the MSCI ACWI, with a beta of 1.46.
  • It is a growth fund, not an income fund: Fidelity showed a 30-day SEC yield of 0.31%.

$FBOT is the Fidelity Disruptive Automation ETF, and here is the short answer to "what am I buying?": about 50 stocks picked by Fidelity's managers, not an index, spread across factory automation, chips, industrial software and a few defense and AI names, for 0.50% a year. It is the only big-name robotics and automation ETF where a human team decides what goes in, and that changes how you should judge it.

I have already done deep dives on BOTZ, ROBO, IBOT, ROBT, ARKQ and the humanoid funds. $FBOT keeps showing up in searches next to them, usually as "fbot stock" or just "fbot", so it gets the same treatment. I went through Fidelity's September 30, 2026 fact sheet, the prospectus and the managers' latest Q&A. Here is what I found.

The basics that matter most:

  • It is actively managed. There is no index. Fidelity's team picks the stocks and decides the weights.
  • It is a converted mutual fund. It launched as the Fidelity Disruptive Automation Fund on April 16, 2020, was reorganized into this ETF on June 9, 2023, and started trading on Nasdaq on June 12, 2023. The ETF kept the mutual fund's track record.
  • It is concentrated. 48 stocks on the September 30 fact sheet, with the top 10 at about 40% of assets.
  • It is global. About 53% US stocks and 47% international, including about 13% in emerging markets.

How FBOT picks its stocks

This is the part most people miss. With $BOTZ, $ROBO, $IBOT or $ROBT, an index decides what you own. With $FBOT, the prospectus gives the managers a mandate instead of a rulebook.

The fund must normally keep at least 80% of its assets in "disruptive automation" companies. Fidelity defines those as companies building new ways to automate tasks and to streamline and scale decision making, and it lists examples: robotics, artificial intelligence, machine vision, process sensors, pneumatic systems, autonomous driving and electric vehicles, and 3D printing. A company can come from any sector.

To count as disruptive, a company has to meet at least one of five tests in the prospectus: a new or evolving business model, being early to market, a competitive advantage, high impact patents, or a long-term outlook that benefits from innovation and research spending. That is a wide door, and it explains why a tractor maker sits near the top of a "robot" fund.

Fidelity describes the process as fundamental research combined with quantitative portfolio construction to manage volatility. The fact sheet lists Michael Kim and Clayton Pfannenstiel among the portfolio managers. In the May 2026 Q&A, Kim describes the fund as low turnover, exiting a few disappointing holdings each year, and the fact sheet shows turnover of just 15% for the year ended May 2026.

Why this matters: With an index fund, you judge the rules. With $FBOT, you are judging the managers. If they are right about which automation companies win, the fund can beat the index funds. If they are wrong, nothing forces the portfolio back toward the theme's leaders. You are paying 0.50% for judgment, so the track record below matters more than it does for an index fund.

The list also moves. StockAnalysis's holdings data as of November 20, 2026 showed 55 positions, with Deere (5.90%) and TSMC (5.88%) on top, followed by PTC, Palantir, Siemens, Nvidia, Emerson Electric, Teradyne, Keyence and Manhattan Associates. Active funds rotate, so check the current list before you buy rather than relying on any snapshot, including this one.

What you will not find near the top are the names many people picture when they hear "robotics ETF": Fanuc, Yaskawa, ABB or Intuitive Surgical. $FBOT leans on the companies that supply and use automation, like machine parts, sensors, chips and industrial software, more than on the robot makers themselves.

Add up the machinery, electrical equipment and instrument lines and roughly half the fund is industrial hardware. That is the real identity of $FBOT: it is closer to an "automated industry" fund than a "robots and AI" fund.

What FBOT costs you

The prospectus shows a 0.50% management fee and 0.50% total annual operating expenses, with no 12b-1 fee. The average across the 30 robotics funds we track is 0.66%. In dollars, $FBOT costs about $50 a year on $10,000. The same $10,000 in $BOTZ at 0.68% costs about $68, in $ROBO at 0.95% about $95, in $ARKQ at 0.75% about $75, and in $IBOT at 0.47% about $47.

For an actively managed fund, 0.50% is cheap. Most active thematic funds charge more than the index funds they compete with, and $FBOT charges less than most of them. My cheapest robotics ETFs guide ranks every option by cost.

Calendar years tell the same story with more color: 20.70% in 2021, -32.49% in 2022, 23.83% in 2023, 12.11% in 2024 and 19.42% in 2025. The MSCI ACWI lost 18.07% in 2022, so $FBOT fell almost twice as hard in the bad year.

Three things jump out. First, against its industrials benchmark, the managers have added value over every period shown. Second, against the broad world stock market, the fund has been hit or miss: ahead over one year, a little behind over three, well behind over five. Third, the ride is rough. Fidelity shows a three year beta of 1.46 against the MSCI ACWI and a standard deviation of 19.20%.

The fall of 2026 is a live example. Fidelity's fact sheet showed $FBOT up 16.49% for the year at the end of September. By the November 20 close at $31.29, StockAnalysis showed the price near its 52 week low of $30.45, down from a high of $40.84, and the fund down about 6% for the year to date. That kind of swing comes with a concentrated, high beta portfolio.

My take: The one year win came mostly from a couple of big semiconductor calls. In the May 2026 Q&A, Kim credited out of index chip names like Teradyne and TSMC for most of the outperformance. That is exactly what you pay an active manager for, but it also shows how much the results depend on a handful of bets going right.

Does FBOT pay a dividend?

Barely. Fidelity showed a 30-day SEC yield of 0.31% as of September 30, 2026. StockAnalysis lists 2026 distributions of $0.030 a share in March and $0.028 in September, roughly a 0.5% trailing yield at today's price. If you want income, this is the wrong fund. If you are an income investor who wants a small growth sleeve, that's fine, just know it won't add to your cash flow. I cover the whole group's payouts in my robotics ETF dividends guide.

How FBOT ranks by size

$FBOT is a small fund for a Fidelity product. The fact sheet showed $213.4 million in portfolio assets at September 30, and StockAnalysis showed about $167 million on November 20 after the price drop. In the May 2026 Q&A, Fidelity reported an average bid and ask spread of 0.17% and average daily volume of about 16,700 shares, so use limit orders. Closure risk looks low with Fidelity behind it, but this is not a giant like $BOTZ.

The real risks

Manager risk

With an active fund, your biggest risk is the people. A few wrong calls in a 50 stock portfolio can cost you, and the five year record shows it can happen.

Concentration

The top 10 is about 40% and the top 20 about 64%. Two or three stocks having a bad quarter can drag the whole fund.

Volatility

A beta of 1.46 and a 32.49% loss in 2022 tell you what a bad year looks like. Size it so a drop like that wouldn't make you sell.

Theme drift

"Disruptive automation" is broad enough to include tractors, body cameras and data software. If you came for robot makers, you may own fewer than you expect. The fund is also non-diversified under SEC rules, which lets it hold bigger positions.

Foreign and cyclical exposure

Nearly half the fund is outside the US, with a meaningful slice in Japan and emerging markets. Much of it is industrial machinery, which tends to follow the economic cycle.

Who FBOT fits, and who it does not

$FBOT may fit you if you want an active manager in the automation theme, you like the industrial and chip tilt, you value a low fee for an active fund, and you're keeping it to a small slice of your portfolio.

$FBOT probably does not fit you if you want a rules based index fund (look at $BOTZ, $IBOT or $ROBO), heavy exposure to humanoid robot makers (see my humanoid robot ETFs guide), the biggest and most liquid fund, or any income at all. If income is your goal, my sister site TopDividendETFs.com covers that side. For a ranked view of every robotics option, see my best robotics ETFs guide.

How I would actually use FBOT

If I owned $FBOT, it would sit next to a broad index fund, not replace one. A total world fund already gives you Nvidia, TSMC, Alphabet and Siemens. What $FBOT adds is a manager's conviction on top: bigger weights in Deere, Teradyne, THK, Keyence and the industrial software names that a market cap index barely notices.

I would keep it small, something like 5% or less, and I would judge it the way you should judge any active fund: once a year, against both the MSCI ACWI and a cheap robotics index fund like $IBOT. If the managers keep adding value over rolling three year periods, the 0.50% is money well spent. If they lag both for a few years, there is no reason to keep paying for judgment.

The other thing I would watch is overlap. If you already own $BOTZ or $ROBT, you will find Nvidia, Keyence and Teradyne in several of your funds. Owning three automation ETFs is not the same as being diversified.

My takeaway

$FBOT is the actively managed, industrial flavored way to own automation. About 50 stocks, a reasonable 0.50% fee, Fidelity's research team, and a portfolio built around the companies that make factories, farms and chip fabs run themselves.

The record is mixed: a strong 2025 and a strong year to September 2026, but a brutal 2022 and a five year return well behind the world market, with about 1.5 times the market's swings. I'd rather own it as a small satellite than a core holding, and I'd compare it with $IBOT and $BOTZ before buying.

Frequently asked questions

What does FBOT hold?

On Fidelity's September 30, 2026 fact sheet, FBOT held 48 stocks. The largest were TSMC (5.73%), Deere (5.43%), Nvidia (5.11%), Teradyne (4.79%) and Palantir (3.85%), followed by Axon, THK, Siemens, Alphabet and Keyence. The top 10 made up about 40% of the fund, and about 47% of assets were in non-US stocks.

What is FBOT's expense ratio?

0.50% a year, or about $50 a year on $10,000. That is cheaper than BOTZ and AIQ at 0.68% and ROBO at 0.95%, and a little more than IBOT at 0.47%. It is low for an actively managed thematic fund.

Is FBOT actively managed?

Yes. FBOT does not track an index. Fidelity's managers pick the stocks using fundamental research combined with quantitative portfolio construction, and the fund must normally keep at least 80% of its assets in disruptive automation companies. Fidelity compares it with the MSCI ACWI.

Does FBOT pay a dividend?

Only a small one. Fidelity showed a 30-day SEC yield of 0.31% as of September 30, 2026, and the 2026 distributions listed by StockAnalysis were about $0.03 a share each. It is a growth fund, not an income fund.

Is FBOT a good investment?

That depends on what you want. FBOT gives you an active manager, a reasonable 0.50% fee and a portfolio that leans on industrial automation and chips rather than pure robot makers. It beat the MSCI ACWI over the year to September 30, 2026 but trailed it over three and five years, with more volatility. It fits best as a small thematic slice. This is not a recommendation.
