KOID vs HUMN: The Two Biggest Humanoid Robot ETFs Compared

KOID owns the parts inside humanoid robots. HUMN owns more of the robot makers. That one design choice explains most of the gap between them. Here is the full comparison with live data.

By Benjie Siegel · Published November 21, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026 · 12 min read

Key takeaways

  • $KOID is the larger fund at $332M and charges 0.69%. $HUMN holds $85M and charges 0.75%.
  • KOID is an index fund with about 59 roughly equal-sized positions. Its ten largest holdings added up to only about 22% of the fund in late October 2026.
  • HUMN is actively managed with about 45 holdings and a much heavier top end: its ten largest lines were about 49% of the fund, including a Treasury bill and a swap on Unitree.
  • One independent count in mid-November 2026 put robot makers at about 29% of HUMN and about 9% of KOID. KOID is mostly chips, sensors and gear makers.
  • Through late August 2026 the two funds were about 30 percentage points apart for the year, with KOID ahead. Construction, not fees, explains it.

Humanoid robots went from science fiction to an investing category in about eighteen months. Two funds launched within weeks of each other in June 2025 to chase it: $KOID from KraneShares on June 4, 2025 and $HUMN from Roundhill on June 26, 2025. They are still the two names most investors compare first.

They share a theme and almost nothing else. One is an index fund, the other is run by a manager. One spreads its money evenly, the other concentrates. One is mostly parts suppliers, the other leans into the companies building the robots. This guide goes through each difference with numbers, and it ends with a tool that lets you line the two up against any other fund on our list. Nothing here is a recommendation.

Size: KOID has pulled well ahead

$KOID holds $332M. $HUMN holds $85M. That makes KOID roughly four times the size, and the largest of the 7 humanoid funds we track.

Size matters for practical reasons. A bigger fund usually trades more shares each day, which tends to mean a tighter gap between the buying and selling price. It also faces less risk of being closed by its issuer. Neither fund is small enough for closure to be a pressing worry, but if you trade in size, KOID's extra liquidity is a real advantage.

One note on the data. You may see much larger asset figures for HUMN quoted in some articles from the summer of 2026. Data providers have disagreed about this fund. The figure in our table comes from our own data sheet, which we check against the issuer.

Fees: close enough not to decide it

KOID charges 0.69% and HUMN charges 0.75%. KraneShares lists a higher gross figure of 0.79% for KOID with a fee waiver bringing the net to 0.69%, so check the fund page for how long that waiver runs.

On $10,000, the gap between the two is about $6 a year. That is not nothing, but it is small next to the difference in what the funds own. For context, the cheapest humanoid fund on our list is $BOTT at 0.35%, about half the price of either.

Construction: the difference that matters most

KOID: equal weight, by the rulebook

KOID tracks an index. The index picks companies tied to humanoid robots and physical AI and gives each one roughly the same weight. In late October 2026 the fund held 59 stocks, and the largest was only about 2.4% of the portfolio. The top ten together were about 22%.

Equal weighting has two effects. No single company can sink the fund. And smaller companies get the same say as giants, so the fund behaves more like a basket of mid-sized industrial and chip names than like a bet on any one robot maker.

HUMN: a manager's concentrated picks

HUMN has no index. Roundhill's managers choose the holdings and size them. In late October 2026 the fund's ten largest lines made up about 49% of assets. That list included a Treasury bill at about 11%, which backs the fund's swap positions, and a swap on Unitree at about 8.7%.

Because it is active, HUMN can move quickly. Its Tesla position is a good example: about 8.9% of the fund in August 2026, about 5.8% at the end of September and about 4.1% in late October. An index fund would not have cut a position that fast between rebalances. Whether that flexibility helps or hurts depends on the manager's calls.

Read down the KOID column and you see chip companies (Texas Instruments, NXP, Analog Devices), sensor and measurement makers (Hexagon, Teledyne, Keyence) and gear and connector suppliers. These are established businesses that would sell parts to whichever robot maker wins. Most of their revenue today comes from other markets.

Read down the HUMN column and you see the robot makers themselves (Unitree, UBTech, Rainbow Robotics, Robotis, Tesla) and the specialist gear makers closest to them (Leader Harmonious Drive, Harmonic Drive Systems). It is a more direct bet on humanoids arriving, and a more volatile one.

A simple way to think about it: KOID owns the picks and shovels. HUMN owns more of the miners. If humanoid robots take off but it is unclear who wins, the parts suppliers get paid either way. If one or two robot makers become giants, the fund that owns more of them benefits most.

Performance: a 30-point gap in 2026

Both funds are too young for long-term conclusions, and we do not publish return rankings. But 2026 produced a striking split that shows how construction plays out in real markets.

According to a 24/7 Wall St. analysis on August 26, 2026, KOID was up about 42% for the year at that point while HUMN was up about 11%. The article traced the gap to HUMN's two largest positions at the time, Tesla at about 8.9% and UBTech at about 6.4%. Both fell sharply mid-year, and HUMN lost about 26% in July alone. KOID took the same July drop but, with nothing above about 2.5%, absorbed it better.

The fall was hard on both. By late October 2026, StockAnalysis showed a one-year return of about 7% for KOID and about -7% for HUMN. A separate report at the end of October put KOID's gain for the year at about 9%, far below its August level.

Two lessons come out of that. First, concentration cuts both ways: the same structure that hurt HUMN in July would have helped if Tesla and UBTech had surged instead. Second, even the more diversified fund gave back most of a 40% gain in two months. Humanoid robotics is a young theme and the stocks swing hard. Our robotics ETF risk guide covers this in more depth.

China exposure

Much of the humanoid supply chain is in China, and both funds reflect that. KOID holds China-listed names such as Unitree and Zhejiang Shuanghuan directly. HUMN reaches some of them through swaps, which is why a Treasury bill sits at the top of its holdings list.

China exposure brings currency moves, different disclosure rules and trade policy risk. Swaps add one more layer: the fund depends on the bank on the other side of the contract. Neither of those is unusual, but both are worth knowing before you buy. If you want a fund built specifically around Chinese robotics, $CROB exists for that.

Dividends

Neither fund is an income investment. KOID yields 0.86% and HUMN yields 0.87%, each paid once a year in December. If income matters to you, see our guide to robotics ETF dividends.

Why investors care about this theme at all

The forecasts are large and uncertain. A Barclays report cited by Zacks in June 2026 projected the humanoid robot market growing to about $200 billion by 2035, from an estimated $2 billion to $3 billion today. Other well-known voices have thrown out figures in the trillions, without firm dates.

Treat all of these as educated guesses. The gap between $3 billion today and $200 billion in a decade is the entire investment case, and nobody knows how fast it closes, which companies capture it or what investors will pay for it along the way.

The bottom line

KOID and HUMN answer the same question in opposite ways. KOID says: own the whole supply chain evenly and let the winners sort themselves out. HUMN says: own more of the robot makers and let a manager steer. In 2026 the first approach held up better, by a wide margin. That is one year, in a theme that has barely started.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between KOID and HUMN?

KOID is an equal-weighted index fund of about 59 companies, mostly the component suppliers behind humanoid robots. HUMN is an actively managed fund of about 45 holdings with larger positions in the companies that build the robots themselves.

Which is bigger, KOID or HUMN?

KOID is the larger fund. See the live table in this guide for current assets of both.

Which is cheaper, KOID or HUMN?

KOID has the slightly lower net expense ratio at 0.69% versus 0.75% for HUMN. On $10,000 that is a difference of about $6 a year.

Does KOID or HUMN own Tesla?

Both have held Tesla. In late October 2026 it was about 2.2% of KOID and about 4.1% of HUMN, down from nearly 9% of HUMN in August.

Do KOID and HUMN own Unitree?

Both have exposure to Unitree, the Chinese humanoid maker that listed in 2026. KOID held the shares at about 2.4% and HUMN held exposure through a swap at about 8.7% in late October 2026.

Is KOID or HUMN better?

Neither is better for everyone. KOID spreads the bet across suppliers. HUMN makes a more concentrated bet on robot makers and can change holdings at the manager's discretion. We do not make buy or sell recommendations.
