KOID ETF Review: Inside the Biggest Humanoid Robot ETF

KOID is the largest humanoid robot ETF. Here is what it owns today, how its equal-weight index works, what it costs, how it has done and who it fits.

By Benjie Siegel · Published November 11, 2026 · Updated November 11, 2026 · 10 min read

Key takeaways

  • $KOID is KraneShares' index fund for humanoid robots and physical AI, with $319M in assets and a 0.69% expense ratio after a fee waiver. It launched on June 4, 2025.
  • It holds 50 stocks, picked by size plus a thematic relevance score and set to equal weight at each quarterly rebalance. No single stock was above 3.5% on November 10, 2026.
  • It is a supply chain fund: actuators, gears, sensors, motors and chips. The US was about 41% and China about 23% of the fund on September 30, 2026.
  • At NAV it returned +5.87% in 2026 through October 31 and +35.45% cumulative since launch, with a rough October (-6.18%).
  • It is not an income fund. KOID has paid one annual distribution so far, about $0.27 a share in December 2025.

$KOID is the biggest humanoid robot ETF, and the short answer to "what am I actually buying?" is this: 50 companies that make the parts inside humanoid robots, from actuators and gears to sensors and chips, held in roughly equal amounts. It holds $319M and charges 0.69% a year.

Humanoid robots are one of the loudest themes in the market right now, and KOID is the fund most people land on when they go looking for a way in. I already reviewed the two biggest classic robotics funds, so it was time to give KOID the same treatment. I went through KraneShares' daily holdings, the August 2026 summary prospectus, the September fact sheet and the fund's performance page. Here is what I found.

The full name is the KraneShares Global Humanoid Robotics and Physical AI Index ETF. It trades on Nasdaq and launched on June 4, 2025. A few basics that matter more than they look:

  • It is passive. KOID tracks an index from MerQube. When it launched, the fund and the index used the phrase "Embodied Intelligence" in their names. Today the fund calls it "Physical AI." Same idea: AI that lives in a body and moves around the real world.
  • It is non-diversified. That is a legal label from the prospectus. It means the fund is allowed to concentrate, though the equal-weight design keeps that in check in practice.
  • It holds 50 stocks. KraneShares listed 50 companies plus a few cash and currency lines on November 10, 2026.
  • It is getting real distribution. KOID was added to Merrill Lynch's platforms in June 2026, and that kind of access is part of why it became the category leader so fast.

How the KOID index picks stocks

This is the part most people skip, and it explains almost everything about how KOID behaves. Based on the summary prospectus, the index works in four steps:

  1. Start with the right industries. It pulls companies from business activity groups tied to humanoid robots, using the Refinitiv Business Classification.
  2. Screen for size and trading. Each stock has to clear a minimum market cap and minimum average daily trading value over one and six months.
  3. Score how relevant each company is. Every company gets a "Thematic Relevance Score" built from its filings, press releases, contracts and news. That score is combined with market cap to rank the list.
  4. Take the top 50 and weight them equally. The index is reconstituted and rebalanced every quarter.
Why equal weight matters: in $BOTZ, the 10 biggest stocks are more than half the fund because it weights by market cap. In KOID, every company starts each quarter at about 2%. You get much more of the small and mid-size parts suppliers and much less of the giants. That is a feature if you want the whole supply chain, and a risk if the small names stumble.

One thing I could not find: the exact market cap and liquidity thresholds. The prospectus describes the screens but does not publish the numbers, so I would not assume every holding is a big, liquid company.

What about Tesla and Nvidia?

This is the part most people miss. Tesla's Optimus robot and Nvidia's robotics chips get most of the headlines, but in KOID they are just two of 50. Tesla was about 2.18% and Nvidia about 1.62% on November 10. On a $10,000 position, that is about $218 of Tesla and $162 of Nvidia. If you want a big Tesla bet on humanoids, this is not that fund.

The US is the biggest slice, but almost a quarter of the fund is in China. That makes sense. China has the deepest supply chain for humanoid components right now, from reducers to servo motors to complete robots like Unitree's. It also brings China-specific risks, which I cover below.

By sector, the fact sheet lists information technology at 42.65%, industrials at 38.66%, consumer discretionary at 13.11% and materials at 5.47%. Roughly 40% chips and sensors, 40% machinery and motion parts, and a slice of automakers and auto parts companies building robots on the side.

What KOID costs you

KOID charges 0.69% a year, but it is worth reading how that number is built. The August 2026 summary prospectus shows a 0.78% management fee plus 0.01% in other expenses, for 0.79% gross. KraneShares waives 0.10% of that until August 1, 2028, which gets you to 0.69% net.

In dollars, that is about $69 a year on $10,000 today, and about $79 if the waiver ends and is not renewed. Not a big gap, but I always want to know when a fee could go up.

The launch timing was good. Most of the gains came in the first several months, when humanoid stocks were rallying hard. 2026 has been choppier: the fund was up 12.84% for the year at the end of September, then October took about 7 points of that away.

Here's what I actually look at with a fund this new: one strong first year tells you almost nothing about the next five. NAV was $33.57 on November 10, 2026. I would assume 30% swings are normal here and size it accordingly.

Does KOID pay a dividend?

Barely. KOID pays once a year. Its only distribution so far went ex on December 22, 2025 and was paid the next day, about $0.27 a share in total. Its yield is about 0.77%.

As someone who reached financial freedom by building dividend income, I'll be blunt: this is a growth bet, not an income tool. If income is your goal, that side of the market lives on my sister site TopDividendETFs.com.

KOID vs HUMN

$HUMN from Roundhill is actively managed, so a person decides what to own. Its top holdings in late September were a T-bill position (about 8.2%), Tesla (about 5.8%), Harmonic Drive (about 5.4%), Unitree through a swap (about 4.9%) and UBTech (about 4.6%). It takes bigger swings on fewer names and costs a bit more. The live numbers are on HUMN vs KOID.

KOID vs BOTT

$BOTT is the cheapest of the three and leans harder into Chinese humanoid companies. On November 11 its top five were Leader Harmonious Drive, Yijiahe, EFORT, UBTech and Tesla, each above 5%. Cheaper, but more concentrated.

KOID vs BOTZ

This is the comparison I get asked about most. I checked the overlap: of the 10 biggest holdings in $BOTZ, only four are in KOID at all (Keyence, Nvidia, Inovance and Cognex), and KOID holds much smaller amounts of each. Intuitive Surgical, Fanuc, ABB, SMC and Daifuku, all big BOTZ positions, are not in KOID. So these two funds really are different bets: BOTZ owns the robots that already sell, KOID owns the parts makers betting on robots that walk. You can see them side by side on BOTZ vs KOID, and my BOTZ ETF review covers the other side in depth.

How KOID ranks by size

KOID had about $336 million on November 10, 2026, per KraneShares. That is well ahead of every other humanoid fund on our list, and size matters in a new category. Small thematic funds get closed when the hype fades. A fund with real assets and advisor platform access is less likely to disappear on you.

The real risks

The theme is early

Very few companies sell humanoid robots in volume today. Most of KOID is betting that parts makers will get a big new market. If humanoids take longer to scale, a lot of these stocks could fall back to being ordinary industrial suppliers, and the theme premium could fade.

China exposure

About 23% of the fund was in China at the end of September, and several top holdings are mainland-listed A shares. That adds regulatory, trade and geopolitical risk. My robotics ETF risks guide goes deeper on this.

Small and mid-size companies

Equal weighting means many holdings are smaller, less liquid companies. They can move a lot more than the giants, in both directions.

Turnover at every rebalance

A quarterly rebalance back to equal weight means selling winners and buying laggards four times a year. That can be healthy discipline, but it also means trading costs that are not in the expense ratio.

Short track record

KOID launched in June 2025. With well under two years of history, there is no long-term record to judge it on.

Who KOID fits, and who it does not

KOID may fit you if you believe in humanoid robots but do not want to guess which company wins, you like a rules-based index over a manager's picks, and you are fine with a big slice of China and smaller companies.

KOID probably does not fit you if you want the established robot makers (look at $BOTZ or $ROBO), you want a concentrated bet on Tesla or a few leaders, you need income, or you cannot handle big swings in a fund with almost no history. For every fund in this space, see my humanoid robot ETF guide.

My takeaway

KOID is the cleanest way I have found to own the whole humanoid robot supply chain without betting on one company. Equal weighting across 50 names means you own the gears, motors, sensors and chips that any humanoid will need, no matter whose robot wins.

The trade-offs are real: a young theme, heavy China exposure, smaller companies and a very short record. I would treat it as a small growth satellite next to a core index fund, and pair it with a classic robotics fund if you want exposure to the robots that already sell today.

Frequently asked questions

What are KOID's top holdings?

As of KraneShares' November 10, 2026 holdings, the largest positions were Unitree Robotics (about 3.4%), Regal Rexnord (about 2.6%), Keli Sensing (about 2.5%), Zhejiang Shuanghuan Driveline (about 2.5%) and NXP (about 2.4%). Because the index is equal weighted at each rebalance, the top 10 changes often and no stock dominates.

What is KOID's expense ratio?

0.69% a year net. The August 2026 summary prospectus shows a 0.78% management fee plus 0.01% other expenses, minus a 0.10% fee waiver that runs until August 1, 2028. That is about $69 a year on $10,000.

Does KOID pay a dividend?

Yes, once a year, and it is small. KOID's only distribution so far was about $0.27 a share, paid December 23, 2025. That works out to a yield of under 1%.

Is KOID a good investment?

It depends on what you want. KOID is a diversified, equal-weight way to own the humanoid robot supply chain, but it is young, volatile and heavy in Chinese stocks. Most investors would treat it as a small growth position, not a core holding. This is not financial advice.

What is the difference between KOID and HUMN?

KOID tracks an equal-weight index of 50 stocks and charges 0.69%. HUMN from Roundhill is actively managed, charges 0.75%, and puts bigger bets on names like Tesla, Harmonic Drive and UBTech.
