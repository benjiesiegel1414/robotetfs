ARKQ ETF Review: What Is Really Inside ARK's Robotics Fund

ARKQ is the best known robotics ETF that barely owns robot makers. Here is what is inside it today, what it costs, how it differs from BOTZ and who it fits.

By Benjie Siegel · Published November 7, 2026 · Updated November 11, 2026 · 10 min read

Key takeaways

  • $ARKQ is an actively managed ARK fund with $1.60B in assets and a 0.75% expense ratio. It has been run by Catherine (Cathie) Wood since it launched on September 30, 2014.
  • It is a very concentrated fund: 36 stocks, with the top 10 making up about 57% of the portfolio and Tesla and SpaceX alone about 22% (ARK holdings file, Nov 6, 2026).
  • ARKQ owns almost none of the classic robot arm makers. Its biggest bets are autonomous driving, space, defense and drones, and AI chips and software.
  • Only 10 of its 36 stocks also show up in $BOTZ, and the weight overlap between the two funds is roughly 17%. They are very different products.
  • ARKQ is a high conviction growth fund, not an income fund. It suits investors who want ARK's view of autonomy and are fine with big swings.

$ARKQ is ARK Invest's autonomy and robotics fund, and the short answer to "what is it really?" is this: it is a concentrated, actively managed bet on self-driving cars, space, defense tech and AI, with only a small slice in traditional robot makers. It holds $1.60B, charges 0.75% a year and owns just 36 stocks.

I wanted to write this review because ARKQ is one of the first funds people find when they search for a robotics ETF, and the name sets an expectation the portfolio does not really match. That is not a knock on the fund. It is just something you should know before you buy it. So I pulled ARK's own daily holdings file, the fund's prospectus filed with the SEC and the holdings of its biggest rival, and went through all of it.

A few basics from the fund's summary prospectus that matter more than they look:

  • It is actively managed. There is no index. ARK's research team decides what goes in and how much, and Catherine D. Wood has been the portfolio manager since the fund launched on September 30, 2014.
  • It is non-diversified. That is a legal term meaning the fund is allowed to put a high percentage of its money into a small number of companies. ARKQ uses that freedom.
  • The goal is long-term growth of capital. Not income, not stability.
  • Turnover was 27% in the fiscal year covered by the November 2025 prospectus, which is moderate for an active fund. ARK does trade, but it is not flipping the whole portfolio every year.

The prospectus also tells you how broad the mandate is. ARKQ invests at least 80% of its assets in what ARK calls autonomous technology and robotics companies, and that definition covers automation and manufacturing, transportation, energy, artificial intelligence and materials. That wide definition is the key to understanding everything below.

Read that list again and count the robot makers. There are none in the top 10. You get the world's most valuable EV company, a rocket company, a defense contractor, an AI software company, two mega-cap tech platforms and a tractor maker.

Each of them has a real autonomy or robotics angle. Tesla is building self-driving software and its Optimus humanoid robot. Alphabet owns Waymo and Amazon owns Zoox, the two big US robotaxi efforts outside Tesla. Deere is pushing autonomous tractors. Kratos builds unmanned jet drones. But these are big, diversified businesses, and their stock prices move on a lot more than robots.

The part most people miss: Tesla and SpaceX together are about 22% of ARKQ. If you buy ARKQ, almost a quarter of your money is riding on two Elon Musk companies. That is a very different risk than "a basket of robotics stocks."

The closest thing to a classic robotics company in the whole fund is Teradyne, which owns the collaborative robot maker Universal Robots, at about 3.2%, along with Symbotic (warehouse robots) and Intuitive Surgical (the da Vinci surgical system). Together that is roughly 7% of the fund.

Meanwhile the nuclear names (Cameco, BWX Technologies, X-energy and Oklo) are in there because ARK's mandate includes energy. Space is in there because ARK sees reusable rockets and satellites as autonomous technology. You can agree or disagree with that framing, but you should know it going in.

ARKQ vs BOTZ: very different funds

The fund most people compare ARKQ to is $BOTZ, the largest dedicated robotics ETF (I break it down in my BOTZ ETF review). I lined up both holdings files from November 6, 2026 to see how much they really have in common.

The answer: not much. Only 10 of ARKQ's 36 stocks also appear in BOTZ. Those shared names are Tesla, Nvidia, Intuitive Surgical, Deere, Symbotic, Aurora Innovation, WeRide, Baidu, Pony AI and Kodiak AI. If you add up the smaller of the two weights for each shared stock, the overlap is only about 17% of the portfolio.

What ARKQ costs you

ARKQ charges 0.75% a year. The prospectus shows a 0.75% management fee and zero other expenses, because ARK pays the fund's other operating costs out of that fee. In plain dollars, at 0.75% you pay about $75 a year for every $10,000 invested. For comparison, the same $10,000 costs about $68 a year in BOTZ at 0.68% and about $47 in $IBOT at 0.47%.

The average fee across the 25 robotics funds we track is 0.67%, so ARKQ is on the expensive side. Here is why that matters over long holding periods.

My honest take: you are paying for active management and ARK's research process. If ARK's picks beat a cheaper index fund, the fee is worth it. If they do not, you are paying more for less. That is the trade-off with every active fund, and it is worth deciding up front which camp you are in. If fees are your top priority, my cheapest robotics ETFs guide lists every low-cost option.

Does ARKQ pay a dividend?

Barely. ARKQ's trailing yield is about 0.27%, and distributions are irregular. Most of the companies it owns either pay no dividend at all (Tesla, SpaceX, Palantir, Rocket Lab) or pay a small one.

As someone who built my own financial freedom around dividend income, I want to be very clear here: ARKQ is not an income tool. Every dollar of return you get from it will come from the share price going up. If you are building a portfolio that pays your bills, this is something you add on the side for growth, not something you lean on. For income ideas, my sister site TopDividendETFs.com tracks the yield side of the market.

The real risks

Concentration in a few names

With 36 stocks and 57% in the top 10, a bad quarter for Tesla or SpaceX can move the whole fund. The prospectus says the fund is concentrated in the industrials and information technology sectors, and the single-stock risk is even bigger than the sector risk.

Manager risk

Because ARKQ is active, you are betting on ARK's judgment as much as on the theme. ARK is known for high conviction calls, and those cut both ways. When ARK is right, the fund can run far ahead of the market. When ARK is wrong, it can fall much further than a broad index.

Valuation and volatility

Many ARKQ holdings are priced for a lot of future growth. Air taxi companies like Archer and Joby, nuclear startups like Oklo and X-energy, and robotaxi companies like Pony AI and WeRide are all early. Expect big price swings, and expect long stretches where the fund lags a plain S&P 500 fund. I go deeper on this in my robotics ETF risks guide.

Theme drift

ARK's mandate is broad enough that the fund can look very different a year from now. Space and nuclear were not what most people pictured when they bought a robotics fund. If you want a fund that stays tightly focused on robots, an index fund with clear rules is more predictable.

China exposure

Baidu, BYD, Pony AI and WeRide add up to a few percent of the fund. Small, but it brings regulatory and geopolitical risk that a US-only fund would not have.

Who ARKQ fits, and who it does not

ARKQ may fit you if you want a concentrated bet on autonomy in the broadest sense (self-driving cars, drones, rockets and the AI behind them), you like ARK's research and want to follow its high conviction picks, and you can sit through large drawdowns without selling.

ARKQ probably does not fit you if you want pure exposure to robot makers, you are fee sensitive, you need income, or you already own a lot of Tesla and Nvidia directly. That last one is easy to overlook. If Tesla and Nvidia are already big positions in your account, ARKQ doubles down on them.

If you want the robot makers themselves, look at $BOTZ, $ROBO or $IBOT. If humanoids are what got you interested, my humanoid robot ETF guide covers $KOID, $HUMN and the rest. And if it is specifically the space side of ARKQ that you like, my sister site TopSpaceETFs.com tracks the dedicated space ETFs that own SpaceX and Rocket Lab.

How ARKQ ranks by size

ARKQ is one of the largest funds in the robotics and autonomy space and has a track record going back to 2014, which is more than almost every newer robotics fund can say. Size and age do not make a fund better, but they do mean it trades easily and has been through full market cycles, including some brutal drawdowns.

My takeaway

ARKQ is a good fund for what it actually is, and a confusing fund for what its name suggests. It is ARK's concentrated, actively managed bet on autonomy across cars, the sky, space and the battlefield, with Tesla and SpaceX at the center and AI chips underneath. It is not a basket of robot arm makers.

Here is what I actually look at before owning something like this: do I agree with the top 10, am I fine with the overlap against what I already own, and am I okay paying 0.75% for someone else's picks? If the answer to all three is yes, ARKQ is a reasonable growth satellite. If not, there are cheaper and more focused options.

Frequently asked questions

What are ARKQ's top holdings?

As of ARK's November 6, 2026 holdings file, the top five were Tesla (about 12.4%), SpaceX (about 9.5%), Nvidia (about 5.7%), Kratos Defense (about 5.1%) and Palantir (about 4.8%). ARK publishes the full list every trading day, so check its site for the current weights.

Does ARKQ own Tesla and SpaceX?

Yes. Tesla was the largest holding at about 12.4% and SpaceX, which went public on Nasdaq in June 2026, was second at about 9.5%. Together they were roughly 22% of the fund.

What is ARKQ's expense ratio?

0.75% a year, according to the fund's prospectus. That is $75 a year on a $10,000 position, a bit more than BOTZ and well above the cheapest robotics funds.

Does ARKQ pay a dividend?

Its yield is tiny and payouts are not regular. ARKQ is built for long-term growth of capital, so almost all of its return depends on the share price.

What is the difference between ARKQ and BOTZ?

ARKQ is actively managed and leans toward autonomous vehicles, space, defense and AI. BOTZ tracks an index and leans toward industrial robot and automation makers like Fanuc, Keyence and ABB. They share only 10 stocks.
