ROBO ETF Review: Inside the Original Robotics ETF

ROBO was the first robotics ETF. Here is what it owns today, how its ROBO Score index works, what the 0.95% fee costs you, how it has done and who it fits.

By Benjie Siegel · Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 21, 2026 · 12 min read

Key takeaways

  • $ROBO is the original robotics ETF, launched in October 2013, with $1.80B in assets and a 0.95% expense ratio, the highest of the big robotics funds.
  • It holds about 80 stocks weighted by a 1 to 100 "ROBO Score," so no stock is big. On November 12, 2026 the largest position was Intuitive Surgical at 2.31% and the top 10 were only about 17% of the fund.
  • That makes it the opposite of $BOTZ, where the top 10 holdings were about 57% of the fund on November 13, 2026.
  • At NAV it returned +8.50% in 2026 through October 31, +10.81% a year over 10 years, but only +2.10% a year over 5 years after a brutal 2022.
  • It is not an income fund. ROBO pays one small distribution a year, about $0.29 a share in December 2025, and skipped 2022 entirely.

$ROBO is the original robotics ETF, and the short answer to "what am I actually buying?" is this: about 80 robotics and automation companies around the world, held in small, similar amounts, with no single stock above about 2.5%. It holds $1.80B and charges 0.95% a year, which makes it the most diversified and the most expensive of the big robotics funds.

I have already done deep dives on BOTZ, ARKQ and KOID, so it was time to give the fund that started the whole category the same treatment. I went through ROBO Global's daily holdings, the August 2026 summary prospectus, the September 30 fact sheet and the fund's distribution history. Here is what I found.

The full name is the ROBO Global Robotics and Automation Index ETF. It trades on NYSE Arca, the adviser is Exchange Traded Concepts, and the index is now owned by VettaFi. A few basics that matter more than they look:

  • It was first. ROBO launched on October 21, 2013, almost three years before $BOTZ. It is the only robotics ETF with more than 10 years of history.
  • It is passive. ROBO tracks the ROBO Global Robotics and Automation Index and fully replicates it, meaning it owns every stock in the index.
  • It is diversified. Unlike most thematic funds, ROBO is legally a diversified fund. It held 79 stocks plus a small cash line on November 12, 2026.
  • It is global by design. The prospectus says the index provider expects at least 40% of the index to be non-US companies under normal conditions.

How the ROBO index picks stocks

This is the part most people skip, and it explains almost everything about how ROBO behaves. Based on the summary prospectus, the index works like this:

  1. Build the universe. ROBO Global keeps a database of robotics and automation companies and sorts them into two buckets. "Technologies" are the companies making the parts, sensors, software and chips. "Applications" are the companies using robotics and automation in their products or factories.
  2. Give each company a ROBO Score. Every company gets a score from 1 to 100 based on how much of its business comes from robotics and automation and how strong its technology and market position are. A score of 50 or more makes it eligible.
  3. Screen for size and trading. Each stock needs a market cap above $200 million and at least $2 million in average daily trading volume over three months when it joins.
  4. Weight by score, not size. Each stock's weight is its ROBO Score as a share of the total score. The index is rebalanced and adds new names every quarter, and there is an ESG screen on top.
Why score weighting matters: Nvidia is one of the biggest companies on earth, but in ROBO it was just 1.01% on November 12, 2026, about the same as Cognex. Because the weights come from a relevance score instead of market cap, the fund never lets a giant take over. You get the whole robotics ecosystem in roughly equal doses, for better and for worse.

The trade-off is turnover. The prospectus reports a 35% portfolio turnover rate for the fiscal year ended April 30, 2026. Trading costs from that turnover are not in the expense ratio, but they still come out of your return.

What about Tesla and Nvidia?

This is the part most people miss. Both are in ROBO, but neither is a big position. Tesla was 1.34% and Nvidia was 1.01% on November 12. On a $10,000 position, that is about $134 of Tesla and $101 of Nvidia. Compare that to $BOTZ, where Nvidia was 6.42% on Global X's November 13 holdings. If you want the AI chip giants to move your fund, ROBO is not built for that.

Humanoid exposure

ROBO does own some of the humanoid robot supply chain: Harmonic Drive (1.41%), Nabtesco (1.29%), UBTech (1.27%), THK (1.16%) and Hiwin (1.33%) all show up. But together that is only a few percent of the fund. If humanoids are the main thing you want, my humanoid robot ETF guide covers the funds built for that.

More than half the fund is outside North America, mostly Japan, Taiwan, Germany and the rest of Europe. And nearly half is in mid and small companies. That is a very different mix from a US large cap index fund, which is the whole point if you want diversification, and the whole risk if foreign and smaller stocks lag.

ROBO Global also splits the fund by its own classification. On September 30, "applications" were 53% (led by manufacturing and industrial automation at 20%, logistics automation at 10%, autonomous systems at 8% and healthcare at 7%) and "technologies" were 47% (led by computing and AI at 16%, actuation at 14% and sensing at 10%).

What ROBO costs you

Here is the honest part. ROBO charges 0.95% a year, and there is no waiver involved. The August 2026 summary prospectus shows a 0.95% management fee and 0.00% in other expenses. The average across the 30 robotics funds we track is 0.66%, so ROBO is well above the pack.

In dollars, that is about $95 a year on $10,000. The prospectus' own example, which assumes a 5% yearly return, puts the total cost at about $525 over 5 years and $1,166 over 10 years on a $10,000 investment. $BOTZ at 0.68% would cost noticeably less, and $IBOT at 0.47% costs about half as much (my IBOT ETF review covers it in depth).

I always tell people fees are the one return drag you can see in advance. A 0.27% gap versus BOTZ does not sound like much, but compounded over 20 years on a growing position it is real money. If cost is your top priority, my cheapest robotics ETFs guide lists every lower-fee option.

How ROBO has performed

ROBO is the one robotics fund with a long record, so this is where it gets interesting. Here are ROBO Global's NAV returns as of October 31, 2026:

That 5 year number is the one to stare at. A five year window that starts near the late 2021 peak includes the 2022 crash in growth and automation stocks. The prospectus shows ROBO's worst quarter was -23.38% in Q2 2022 and its best was +28.80% in Q2 2020. That is the kind of ride this fund gives you.

Now compare it to the broad market. For the 10 years ended December 31, 2025, the prospectus shows ROBO returned 11.26% a year, while the S&P 500 returned 14.82% a year. Over 5 years it was 2.84% versus 14.42%. So for all the excitement around robotics, the original robotics ETF has trailed a plain S&P 500 index fund over the long run.

Here's what I actually look at: the 3 year number (+18.16% a year) shows the theme can run hard when it is in favor, but the 5 and 10 year numbers show it does not reliably beat the market. I would not buy ROBO expecting it to replace an index fund. I would buy it, if at all, as a satellite on top of one.

ROBO vs BOTZ

This is the matchup I get asked about most, and the difference is concentration. On Global X's November 13, 2026 holdings, BOTZ's top 10 were about 57% of the fund, led by Intuitive Surgical (10.55%), Fanuc (8.50%), Keyence (8.42%), ABB (7.75%) and Nvidia (6.42%). ROBO owns all five of those, but at 1% to 2.3% each. BOTZ is a bet on the leaders. ROBO is a bet on the whole field. I break this down further in BOTZ vs ROBO.

ROBO vs ARKQ

$ARKQ is actively managed and runs a far more concentrated portfolio built around autonomy and disruptive tech. ROBO is the opposite: rules based and spread thin. My ARKQ ETF review covers that fund in detail, and the numbers sit side by side on ARKQ vs ROBO.

ROBO vs IBOT

$IBOT from VanEck is much smaller but costs about half as much. If you like ROBO's idea of a broad robotics basket but hate the fee, it is worth a look on IBOT vs ROBO.

How ROBO ranks by size

ROBO had about $1.83 billion in net assets on November 12, 2026, per ROBO Global. That makes it one of the largest robotics ETFs, behind only $BOTZ among the pure robotics funds. Size matters with thematic ETFs: big, established funds are far less likely to be shut down when a theme cools off. Its median 30 day bid/ask spread was 0.34%, which is fine for a long-term holder but worth using limit orders for.

The real risks

A high fee that compounds

At 0.95%, ROBO starts every year about a point behind its index. You can see that in the fact sheet: through September 30, 2026, the fund's 10 year return was 11.29% a year versus 12.15% for its index.

It has lagged the market

As covered above, ROBO trailed the S&P 500 by a wide margin over 5 and 10 years. A theme being exciting does not mean the stocks beat the market.

Cyclical industrial exposure

A big chunk of ROBO is factory automation, machinery and logistics. Those companies sell to manufacturers, and when factories cut spending, orders drop fast. The prospectus notes the fund was concentrated in the industrial machinery and equipment industry as of August 2026.

Foreign and smaller companies

With more than half the fund outside North America and nearly half in mid and small caps, ROBO carries currency risk, foreign market risk and more volatility than a US large cap fund. It can also hold China A shares, which bring their own trading and policy risks. My robotics ETF risks guide goes deeper on all of this.

Diluted upside

Spreading money across 80 names protects you from one blowup, but it also means one big winner barely moves the fund. If Nvidia doubles, ROBO feels about 1%.

Who ROBO fits, and who it does not

ROBO may fit you if you want the broadest, most balanced robotics and automation basket in one ticker, you like a long track record and a big, established fund, and you are fine paying up for that diversification.

ROBO probably does not fit you if fees are your first filter (look at $IBOT or $BOTZ), you want concentrated exposure to the leaders, you want a pure humanoid bet, or you need income. For a ranked view of every option, see my best robotics ETFs guide.

My takeaway

ROBO is the most diversified robotics ETF I have reviewed. About 80 stocks, small even weights, a global mix and more than a decade of history. If you want to own "automation" as a whole rather than guess which company wins, it does that job well.

The catch is the price. At 0.95% it is the most expensive of the big robotics funds, and its long-term record trails a plain S&P 500 fund. I would rather own it as a small satellite next to a low-cost core index fund than as a main holding, and I would compare it carefully against cheaper broad options before buying.

Frequently asked questions

What are ROBO's top holdings?

On ROBO Global's November 12, 2026 holdings, the largest positions were Intuitive Surgical (2.31%), PTC (1.91%), Fanuc (1.75%), Emerson Electric (1.74%) and Rockwell Automation (1.71%). The fund held 79 stocks plus cash, and the top 10 added up to only about 17%.

What is ROBO's expense ratio?

0.95% a year. The August 2026 summary prospectus shows a 0.95% management fee with no other expenses and no fee waiver. That is about $95 a year on $10,000, and the prospectus estimates about $1,166 in costs over 10 years on $10,000 assuming a 5% yearly return.

Does ROBO pay a dividend?

Yes, once a year in December, and it is small. ROBO paid about $0.29 a share in December 2025 and about $0.31 in December 2024. It paid nothing in 2022. The yield is well under 1%.

Is ROBO ETF a good investment?

It depends on what you want. ROBO is the most diversified way to own the robotics and automation theme, with about 80 stocks and no big single bets, but it is the most expensive of the large robotics ETFs and has lagged the S&P 500 over the last 5 and 10 years. This is not financial advice.

What is the difference between ROBO and BOTZ?

ROBO spreads money across about 80 stocks weighted by a ROBO Score, so the top 10 are about 17% of the fund, and charges 0.95%. BOTZ holds fewer stocks weighted by market cap, so its top 10 were about 57% of the fund, and it charges 0.68%.
