{{link:IBOT}} is the VanEck Robotics ETF, and the short answer to "what am I actually buying?" is this: about 70 robotics and automation companies, with a big tilt toward the tech side of the theme, like factory software, machine vision and chip equipment, for {{exp:IBOT}} a year. That fee is about half of what {{link:ROBO}} charges, which is the main reason people find this fund.

I have already done deep dives on BOTZ, ROBO, ARKQ and KOID. IBOT is the small, cheap one that keeps showing up in people's searches next to them, so it gets the same treatment. IBOT at a glance

{{table_of:IBOT,ROBO,BOTZ,ARKQ}}

The full name is the VanEck Robotics ETF. It trades on Nasdaq, it launched on April 5, 2023, and VanEck showed {{aum:IBOT}} in total net assets as of November 13, 2026. A few basics that matter more than they look:

  • It is passive. IBOT tracks the BlueStar Robotics Index. It does not have a manager picking stocks.
  • It is young. With about three and a half years of history, it has never lived through a full robotics bear market like 2022.
  • It is small. At around $100 million, it is a fraction of the size of {{link:BOTZ}} or ROBO. That matters for trading and for how long a fund sticks around.
  • It is cheap for the category. VanEck lists a 0.47% management fee and no other expenses, so 0.47% all in.

How the BlueStar Robotics Index picks stocks

This is the part that explains why IBOT looks different from the other robotics funds. In VanEck's own Q&A on the fund, the index is described like this:

  1. Revenue test. A company has to get at least 50% of its revenue from one or more of seven robotics subthemes.
  2. Seven subthemes. Robots and industrial automation systems, 3D printing, robotics and manufacturing software (like CAD), machine vision, robotic surgery, semiconductor manufacturing systems, and embedded machine learning chips.
  3. Three tiers. VanEck described the index as putting 50% in tier one (robots, industrial automation and 3D printing), 25% in tier two (software and machine vision) and 25% in tier three (surgical robots, chip equipment and machine learning chips).
Why this matters: Because software, machine vision and chip equipment get their own built-in share of the index, IBOT ends up far more "tech" than you might expect from a fund called Robotics. On November 13, 2026, VanEck's sector breakdown of the holdings was about 51% information technology and 44% industrials. You are buying the brains and the tools of automation as much as the robot arms.

The tier weights are how VanEck explained the index when the fund launched, so I treat them as the design intent rather than a live number. The live holdings below are what you actually own today.

Two things stand out to me. First, the tracking is tight. The fund trails its index by a bit less than a percent a year, which is about what you'd expect from a 0.47% fee plus trading costs. Second, the record is short and it is all from a good stretch for automation stocks. IBOT launched after the 2022 crash. ROBO, which did live through it, had a worst quarter of -23.38% in 2022. I'd assume IBOT can fall that hard too, because it owns many of the same kinds of companies.

Even with a strong start, I would not buy a robotics ETF expecting it to replace a broad index fund. I'd treat it as a satellite on top of one.

Does IBOT pay a dividend?

Barely. VanEck lists the distribution frequency as annual, with a 0.33% distribution yield and a 0.67% 30-day SEC yield as of November 2026. On $10,000 that is roughly $33 to $67 a year.

As someone who reached financial freedom by building dividend income, I'll be blunt: this is a growth fund, not an income tool. If income is what you're after, that side of the market lives on my sister site TopDividendETFs.com.

How IBOT ranks by size

This is IBOT's weak spot. At about $100 million, it is small next to BOTZ and ROBO. Small funds tend to have wider bid and ask spreads, so I'd use limit orders when buying or selling. And thematic funds that never grow are the ones that get closed. I don't see any sign of that here, and VanEck runs plenty of niche funds, but size is worth watching.

The real risks

Short track record

IBOT has only been around since April 2023. Every return number above comes from a strong period for automation stocks, so you have not seen how this fund behaves in a real downturn.

Tech and chip sensitivity

With about half the fund in information technology, including chip equipment and AI chips, IBOT will move with the semiconductor cycle more than a pure robot maker fund would. When chip stocks sell off, expect IBOT to feel it.

Cyclical industrial exposure

The other half is industrial automation. Those companies sell to factories, and when factories cut spending, orders drop fast.

Foreign stocks and currency

More than half the fund is outside the US, with over 20% in Japan. A strong dollar or a weak yen can drag on returns even when the businesses do fine. My robotics ETF risks guide goes deeper on this.

A small fund

Lower assets mean lower trading volume and a higher chance the fund never reaches the scale of its rivals.

Who IBOT fits, and who it does not

IBOT may fit you if you want robotics and automation exposure at one of the lowest fees in the category, you like the "picks and shovels" side of automation (software, sensors, chip tools) as much as the robot makers, and you are fine owning a younger, smaller fund.

IBOT probably does not fit you if you want Tesla or a humanoid robot bet, you want the big surgical robot weight that BOTZ gives you, you want the longest track record (that is ROBO), or you need income. For a ranked view of every option, see my best robotics ETFs guide.

My takeaway

IBOT is the value pick of the core robotics funds. At 0.47% it costs about half what ROBO does, it tracks its index closely, and its first few years have been strong. What you get for that price is a robotics fund that leans hard into automation software, machine vision and chip equipment, with less of the surgical robot and Tesla exposure people often expect.

The catches are size and history. It is a small fund with a short record that has not been tested by a bad market. I'd rather own it as a small satellite next to a low-cost core index fund than as a main holding, and I'd compare it side by side with BOTZ before buying.
