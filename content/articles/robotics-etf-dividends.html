Why robotics ETFs yield so little

The companies are reinvesting

An ETF can only pass through what its holdings pay. Robotics and AI companies are spending heavily on research, new factories and acquisitions. Young humanoid makers are not profitable at all. There is little left to hand out.

Growth stocks trade at high prices relative to dividends

Even the holdings that do pay, such as large industrial automation companies, tend to trade at prices that make their dividend look small as a percentage.

Fees come out first

A fund's expenses are paid out of the income it collects before anything reaches you. With fees on our list averaging {{avg_exp}}, a fund whose holdings yield 1% in total has very little left to distribute. That is why some funds with real dividend-paying holdings still show yields near zero.

{{chart:exp}}

Some funds use swaps

Funds that get exposure through swaps instead of owning shares do not receive dividends from those stocks in the usual way. What they distribute comes mostly from interest on their collateral.

What a 0.00% yield really means

Several funds on our list show a yield of zero. There are three different reasons, and they are not equally permanent.

  • The fund is new. Funds such as {{link:RBTX}}, {{link:TORK}}, {{link:RTOO}}, {{link:HOID}} and {{link:ROBX}} launched in 2026 and have not reached their first scheduled distribution. Most will declare something small in December.
  • The holdings pay nothing. Drone and early-stage robot companies generally do not pay dividends, so funds such as {{link:JEDI}} and {{link:DRNZ}} have little to pass along.
  • The fund does not distribute regularly. A few funds, such as {{link:IQM}} and {{link:LOUP}}, have not made regular income distributions.

Yield is not return

For a growth fund, the dividend is a rounding error next to the change in share price. A fund that yields 0.5% and rises 15% returned far more than a fund that yields 2% and falls 10%. The number that matters is total return: price change plus distributions.

The reverse is also true, and it is a point we make across our income sites. A high payout does not make up for a falling share price. If you would not own a fund without its dividend, the dividend is not a good reason to own it.

How income investors usually handle robotics

If your portfolio is built for cash flow, a robotics fund will not contribute much. These are the common ways people fit one in. None is a recommendation.

1. Growth sleeve next to an income core

The most common approach is to keep the income holdings doing their job and add robotics as a separate, smaller growth position. You judge it on total return and do not expect it to pay bills.

2. Reinvest the dividends from income funds into growth

Some investors direct part of the cash their income funds throw off into a growth theme each month. That builds the position gradually without selling anything, and it spreads purchases across many prices.

3. Sell a small slice on a schedule

If you need cash from a growth holding, selling a fixed small percentage once or twice a year creates a homemade dividend. You control the amount and timing. In a taxable account each sale is a taxable event, so it is worth understanding the tax side first.

4. Pair with an option income fund

There are option income ETFs built on technology and semiconductor indexes that pay monthly or weekly. They are not robotics funds, but they share some holdings with the AI and robotics category. You can browse them on TopDividendETFs.com, WeeklyETFs.com and MonthlyETFs.com.

Taxes in brief

We are not tax advisors. A few general points help you ask the right questions.

  • ETF distributions are reported on Form 1099-DIV. Part may be qualified dividends, taxed at lower rates if you meet holding period rules, and part may be ordinary income.
  • Funds with many foreign holdings may pass through foreign taxes paid, which can sometimes be claimed as a credit.
  • Funds occasionally distribute capital gains in December. This is less common for ETFs than for mutual funds but it does happen, especially with actively managed and leveraged funds.
  • In an IRA or other retirement account, distributions are generally not taxed in the year they are paid.

How to check a fund's payout history

  1. Open the fund's profile on this site and follow the link to the issuer's page.
  2. Find the distributions tab. It lists each payment with its ex-dividend date, pay date and amount per share.
  3. Add up the last twelve months and divide by the current price to get the trailing yield yourself.
  4. Look at how steady the payments have been. A single large payment followed by tiny ones is a sign the yield is not repeatable.
  5. Compare the 30-day SEC yield, if shown, with the trailing yield. The SEC yield is a standardized estimate based on recent income after expenses.

The bottom line

Robotics ETFs pay dividends in the technical sense. Most yield under 1%, many pay once a year and a good number of the newest funds have not paid at all yet. That is normal for a group of companies that are still building.
