{{link:HUMN}} is the Roundhill Humanoid Robotics ETF, and the short answer to "what am I buying?" is this: a hand picked, concentrated basket of about 38 companies building humanoid robots and the parts inside them, with roughly half the money in China, for {{exp:HUMN}} a year. It is the most aggressive way to bet on robots that walk, and it trades like it.

I have already done deep dives on KOID, BOTZ, ROBO, ARKQ and IBOT. HUMN keeps showing up in searches for humanoid robotics ETFs, so it gets the same treatment. I went through Roundhill's full daily holdings, its fund details, its performance and distribution data, and the launch notes that explain how the fund is run. Here is what I found.

HUMN at a glance

{{table_of:HUMN,KOID,BOTT,BOTZ}}

Roundhill calls HUMN the first US listed humanoid ETF. It began trading on Cboe BZX on June 26, 2025, and Roundhill showed about $78.8 million in assets and 38 holdings on its fund page this week. The basics that matter most:

  • It is active. There is no index. Roundhill's investment committee decides what to own and how much.
  • It is young. About 17 months of history, all of it during the humanoid hype cycle.
  • It is small. Under $100 million is a fraction of {{link:BOTZ}} and well behind {{link:KOID}}.
  • It has options. Roundhill lists HUMN as optionable, which is unusual for a fund this size.

How HUMN picks its stocks

This is the big difference between HUMN and the other humanoid funds. KOID follows an index that resets to equal weight every quarter. HUMN has people making the calls.

In Roundhill's launch note, the pitch was simple: humanoid robotics moves faster than index rebalancing schedules, so an active manager can buy newly public companies early, trim names when momentum fades and size up a supplier that lands a big contract. Roundhill also said it wanted to skip "filler" companies whose humanoid work is a side project.

Why this matters: Active management cuts both ways. It let HUMN own Agility Robotics within days of its Nasdaq debut and hold Unitree through a swap. It also means your results depend on a small team's judgment, and the portfolio you buy today may look very different in six months. You are buying a manager's view of the theme, not the theme itself.

What HUMN costs you

In dollars, that is about $75 a year on $10,000. The same $10,000 in KOID at 0.69% costs about $69, and in {{link:BOTT}} at 0.35% about $35. A $40 a year gap versus BOTT doesn't sound like much. Over decades, it compounds.

So the fund made money since launch, but almost all of it came early. Over the 12 months to September 30 it was slightly down, a stretch when plenty of robotics funds were up double digits.

And it swings hard. On November 17, 2026, HUMN closed at $24.76, down 4.09% on the day at market price and 4.18% at NAV. That kind of daily move is normal for a fund packed with small Chinese and Korean robot makers. If a 4% day makes you want to sell, this fund will test you often.

I'd never buy a fund like this expecting it to replace a broad index fund. At most, it is a small satellite position on top of one.

Does HUMN pay a dividend?

Barely. Roundhill lists the distribution frequency as annual. The only payment so far was $0.215601 a share at the end of December 2025, and the 30-day SEC yield was 0.36% as of October 31, 2026. Roundhill's calendar shows the next distribution window at the end of December 2026.

As someone who built financial freedom on dividend income, I'll be blunt: nobody should own HUMN for income. If income is your goal, my sister site TopDividendETFs.com is where that side of the market lives.

Small thematic funds also carry closure risk if assets never grow. I don't see signs of that here, and Roundhill has been adding to its lineup, but it is worth watching.

The real risks

China concentration

About half the fund is tied to Chinese companies. Trade restrictions, tariffs, delisting rules or a policy shift in Beijing can hit these stocks all at once, no matter how good the robots are.

Swap and counterparty risk

The largest position is a swap, not shares. A swap depends on the bank on the other side of the contract, and it can be more expensive or harder to hold if markets get stressed.

Hype and valuation

Most humanoid companies sell very few robots today. Prices are driven by expectations, and expectations can reset fast. The flat one year return shows how quickly the excitement can cool.

Manager risk

With no index, a few people decide everything. Good calls can beat an index. Bad ones can lag it badly.

Short history and small size

Seventeen months is not a track record, and the fund has never been through a real bear market. My robotics ETF risks guide covers these issues across the whole category.

Who HUMN fits, and who it does not

HUMN may fit you if you want the purest, most concentrated humanoid bet available, you want exposure to names like Unitree and Agility that are hard to buy directly, you're comfortable with heavy China exposure, and you're keeping it to a small slice of your portfolio.

HUMN probably does not fit you if you want a diversified, rules based fund (look at KOID), the lowest fee (look at BOTT), established robotics companies with real revenue (look at BOTZ or ROBO), or anything resembling income. For a ranked view of every option, see my best robotics ETFs guide.

My takeaway

HUMN is a high conviction, actively managed bet on humanoid robots. Its edge is access: a big Unitree position through a swap, Agility Robotics days after it listed, and a deep bench of Chinese, Korean and Japanese parts makers. Its costs are concentration, China exposure, a 0.75% fee and a short, bumpy record.

I'd rather own it as a small satellite than a core holding, and I'd look at it side by side with KOID before buying. If you believe humanoids are the next big wave and want a manager steering through it, HUMN is the most direct way in. If you just want the theme, KOID or BOTT gets you there with less single stock risk.
