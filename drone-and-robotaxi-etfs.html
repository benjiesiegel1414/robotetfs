Drone and Robotaxi ETFs: Investing in Machines That Move on Their Own

Self-driving cars and drones are robotics on the move. Here is a deep look at the ETFs focused on autonomy, what drives these businesses, how the funds differ, and the risks that set them apart from classic robotics funds.

Key takeaways

  • Four funds on our list focus on autonomy: $CABZ (robotaxis), $DRNZ (drones), $WDRN (physical AI including drones) and $ARKQ (autonomy, robotics and more).
  • $ARKQ is by far the largest and oldest, with $1.60B and a launch date of September 30, 2014. The dedicated drone and robotaxi funds are much newer and smaller.
  • Drone stocks are closely tied to defense budgets. Robotaxi stocks are tied to regulation and a few large automakers and tech firms.
  • Autonomy funds are narrower than classic robotics funds, so expect bigger swings.

Not every robot works in a factory. Some fly and some drive. Drones and self-driving vehicles are two of the fastest-moving areas of robotics, and they share the same core technology as industrial and humanoid robots: sensors that perceive the world, AI that decides what to do, and motors that act on it. The difference is that these machines move freely through open, unpredictable environments.

A small group of ETFs now targets autonomy directly. This guide covers each one, explains what drives the businesses inside them, and lays out how these funds differ from classic robotics funds like $BOTZ and $ROBO.

What makes something "autonomous"

An autonomous machine has to do three things on its own: perceive its surroundings with cameras, radar, lidar and other sensors; decide what to do with onboard computing and AI models; and act through motors, steering and flight controls. Each layer is a business opportunity, which is why autonomy funds hold a mix of vehicle makers, chip companies, sensor makers and software firms.

Robotaxis: $CABZ

Roundhill's $CABZ targets companies building autonomous vehicles and robotaxi services, from the vehicle platforms to the sensors, chips and software behind them. It launched on January 14, 2026 and holds $2.8M, with a 0.59% expense ratio.

What drives robotaxi stocks: the pace of driverless service launches in new cities, safety records, regulation at the state and federal level, and the economics of running a fleet without drivers. A handful of very large automakers and technology companies dominate the space, so a robotaxi fund's results can hinge on a few names.

Watch for: concentration in a few big holdings, and regulatory news that can move the whole group at once.

Drones: $DRNZ

REX's $DRNZ focuses on companies building drones and unmanned systems for defense, commercial and consumer use. It holds $83M and charges 0.65%.

What drives drone stocks: military demand is the biggest factor. Modern conflicts have shown how important small, cheap unmanned systems have become, and governments have increased spending on them. Commercial uses such as inspection, agriculture, mapping and delivery are growing too, but defense contracts still drive much of the revenue.

Watch for: sensitivity to defense budgets, contract announcements and geopolitical news. A drone ETF can trade more like a defense fund than a technology fund.

Physical AI mix: $WDRN

WisdomTree's $WDRN combines drones with humanoids and other physical AI. It launched on May 14, 2026 with a 0.45% fee and holds $15M. It suits investors who want exposure to several kinds of autonomous machines in one fund rather than a single niche.

The veteran: $ARKQ

ARK's $ARKQ is actively managed and covers autonomous vehicles, robotics, 3D printing, energy storage and space exploration. It launched on September 30, 2014, holds $1.60B and charges 0.75%. ARK is known for concentrated, high-conviction portfolios, so a few large positions can drive results. It is the most established way to get autonomy exposure, but it is broader than a pure drone or robotaxi fund. For a full breakdown of what it owns today, see my ARKQ ETF review.

How autonomy funds differ from classic robotics funds

Main customersFactories, warehouses, hospitalsConsumers, cities, militaries
Key driversIndustrial spending, labor costs, AI chipsRegulation, defense budgets, fleet economics
Track recordMany yearsMostly new funds
ConcentrationModerateOften high
Typical fund sizeBillionsMillions

The practical result: autonomy funds are narrower bets. They can move sharply on a single regulatory decision or defense budget, while classic robotics funds tend to follow broader industrial and tech trends.

Risks specific to drone and robotaxi ETFs

  • Regulation: driverless vehicles and drone flights are heavily regulated. Rule changes can open or close markets quickly.
  • Safety events: a single high-profile accident can hit the whole robotaxi group.
  • Defense dependence: drone makers can be exposed to budget cuts or contract delays.
  • Small fund size: newer autonomy funds can trade with wider spreads and carry closure risk.
  • Overlap: large automakers and chip companies also appear in broad tech and AI funds you may already own.

The bottom line

Autonomy is one of the most exciting slices of robotics and one of the most volatile. $ARKQ offers the longest track record, $CABZ and $DRNZ offer pure exposure to robotaxis and drones, and $WDRN blends autonomy with humanoids. Frequently asked questions

Is there a drone ETF?

Yes. DRNZ from REX Shares focuses on drone and unmanned systems companies, and WDRN from WisdomTree mixes drones with humanoids and physical AI.

Is there a robotaxi ETF?

CABZ from Roundhill targets robotaxi and autonomous vehicle companies. ARKQ from ARK also holds autonomy and robotics names as part of a broader strategy.

Are drone ETFs defense funds?

Partly. Many drone makers depend on military contracts, so drone ETFs can behave like defense funds and react to defense budget news.

Do robotaxi ETFs own Tesla?

Funds focused on autonomous driving often hold large automakers and technology companies working on self-driving. Check each fund's current holdings on the issuer's site.

Are autonomy ETFs riskier than robotics ETFs?

They are usually narrower, which can mean bigger swings. Their results depend heavily on a few themes like regulation and defense spending.
